Lower sample rates need a low-pass filter before resampling, removing frequencies that cannot be represented. Lossy codecs can also remove or approximate information within the remaining bandwidth. Spectra are short-window estimates, not quality scores.

BITRATE ≠ SAMPLE RATE

Two different dials.

Sample rate counts measurements per second, in Hz. Bitrate counts stored bits per second, in bit/s. Uncompressed PCM bitrate = sample rate × bit depth × channels. Here, 44,100 × 16 × 2 = 1,411,200 bit/s, or 1,411.2 kbps, before headers.

For MP3 and AAC, bitrate is an encoder budget. Lowering it can make a smaller file at the same sample rate, using a different representation of the sound.

LOSSLESS ≠ UNCOMPRESSED

Smaller can sound identical.

FLAC compresses the PCM data reversibly. At the source’s sample rate, every decoded sample matches the WAV. More compression effort can change file size without changing the sound. WAV here contains uncompressed PCM; a file extension alone does not describe every possible encoding.

MP3 and AAC use lossy encoding. A high bitrate may sound indistinguishable to you, while its decoded samples still differ.

LOSSLESS HAS A REFERENCE

You cannot undo a discarded detail.

Reducing sample rate first discards information—even if the reduced-rate signal is then saved as WAV or FLAC. FLAC can preserve that new signal exactly, but cannot restore what the resampling removed. Converting an MP3 to FLAC cannot recover the original either.

How was losslessness checked?

During preparation, every WAV and FLAC was decoded to 16-bit PCM and checked byte for byte against the WAV at its own sample rate. Only a match at 44.1 kHz also matches the original source. All FLAC effort settings at the same rate reconstruct the same PCM data.
